# Difficult Conversations

## Purpose

Difficult conversations — scope disputes, dissatisfaction, missed expectations, payment issues — are inevitable in freelance work. Handled well, they often strengthen trust rather than end the relationship.

---

# Principles

* Address the issue directly and promptly; avoiding it makes things worse.
* Separate the problem from the person — stay factual, not defensive or accusatory.
* Acknowledge legitimate concerns before explaining your position.
* Always propose a path forward, not just an explanation of what happened.
* Put resolutions in writing once agreed.

---

# Client Is Unhappy With the Work

1. Ask specific questions to understand exactly what's wrong — don't assume.
2. Acknowledge the gap between expectation and delivery, even if scope was technically followed.
3. Propose a concrete fix or revision path.
4. Confirm the revised understanding in writing before continuing.

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> I want to make sure this gets to where you need it. Could you walk me through specifically what's not working as expected? I'd like to understand exactly what you're seeing before proposing a fix.

---

# Client Requests Work Outside Agreed Scope

Distinguish between a misunderstanding and deliberate scope creep, then respond factually rather than defensively.

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> Happy to take this on — just want to flag that [item] falls outside what we originally scoped in [agreement/proposal]. I can add it as an additional item at [rate/estimate], or we can look at adjusting the current scope to make room for it. Which works better for you?

---

# Client Is Slow to Pay

Stay factual and professional; avoid accusatory language even when frustrated.

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> Following up on invoice [#], which was due on [date]. Let me know if there's anything blocking payment on your end, or if I should resend it in a different format.

If non-payment continues, escalate professionally through the platform's or contract's formal process rather than informal pressure.

---

# Client Is Unresponsive Mid-Project

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> I haven't heard back on [specific pending item] in a while, and it's currently blocking progress on [task]. Let me know if there's a better time to reconnect, or if priorities have shifted — happy to adjust the plan either way.

If unresponsiveness continues significantly, document the blocker and communicate its impact on timeline clearly rather than silently absorbing the delay.

---

# Need to Push Back on an Unrealistic Request

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> I want to be upfront rather than agree to something I don't think is realistic: [specific concern, e.g., timeline, scope, technical approach]. Here's what I'd recommend instead: [alternative].

Honesty here protects the relationship long-term more than agreeing and underdelivering.

---

# Ending an Engagement That Isn't Working

When a relationship genuinely isn't working despite good-faith attempts to resolve it:

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> After [reflecting on recent challenges], I don't think I'm the right fit to continue this project effectively. I want to make sure the transition is as smooth as possible — I can [hand off documentation/code, complete the current milestone, recommend next steps] before wrapping up.

Always offer a professional, clean handoff rather than disappearing — reputation matters beyond any single engagement.

---

# What to Avoid

* Responding defensively or emotionally, even when the criticism feels unfair.
* Avoiding the conversation and hoping the issue resolves itself.
* Making promises under pressure that aren't realistic.
* Burning the relationship over a single difficult moment when a professional resolution is possible.

---

# Guiding Principle

The goal of a difficult conversation is resolution and clarity, not being right. Handled well, these moments often build more trust than smooth projects do.
