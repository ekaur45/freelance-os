# Negotiation

## Purpose

Negotiation should protect fair value for the work while remaining flexible on how that value is delivered. The goal isn't to "win" against the client — it's to reach terms both sides can commit to without resentment.

---

# Core Principles

* Compete on value, expertise, and reliability — not on being the cheapest option.
* Never discount simply to avoid losing a client; discount only when scope, timeline, or risk genuinely decreases.
* Be willing to walk away from terms that don't work. A bad engagement costs more than a lost opportunity.
* Get any negotiated agreement confirmed in writing before starting work.

Refer to `Knowledge/08_Sales/SalesPlaybook.md` for the broader sales philosophy this sits within.

---

# When a Client Pushes Back on Price

Options, in order of preference:

1. **Reduce scope** to fit the budget while keeping the rate/quality intact.
2. **Adjust timeline** (longer delivery window can sometimes offset a tighter budget).
3. **Phase the project** — deliver a smaller first phase within budget, with later phases scoped separately.
4. **Discount modestly** only if it reflects reduced risk (e.g., simpler requirements than initially assumed, a longer-term relationship, or a portfolio-worthy project).

Avoid matching a lower number just to win the job without adjusting scope — it sets a bad precedent for the entire engagement.

---

# When a Client Wants to Negotiate Scope After Price Is Set

* Distinguish between clarifying existing scope (free) and adding new scope (billable).
* Use the original proposal or contract as the reference point for what was agreed.
* When scope creep happens gradually, name it directly and propose a change order or updated estimate rather than absorbing it silently.

---

# When a Client Wants Unlimited Revisions or Open-Ended Scope

* Explain why open-ended scope makes fixed pricing impossible to sustain.
* Offer a defined number of revision rounds within the fixed price, with additional rounds billed separately.
* For genuinely undefined projects, recommend hourly or milestone-based pricing instead of a single fixed quote.

---

# Negotiating Timeline

* Be honest about what's actually achievable — overcommitting to win a negotiation damages trust more than losing the negotiation would.
* If a client needs a faster timeline than proposed, discuss what that requires (reduced scope, additional cost for prioritized time, or accepting some risk).

---

# Negotiating Payment Terms

* A deposit or milestone-based payment structure is reasonable to request, especially for new clients or larger fixed-price projects.
* Be flexible on payment schedule (e.g., weekly vs. milestone) but not on the principle of being paid for completed work before continuing to new work.
* For long engagements, avoid a single payment at the very end — structure payments around delivered milestones.

---

# Recognizing a Negotiation That Should End

Walk away when:

* The client wants to negotiate price down while also expanding scope.
* The client treats the negotiation disrespectfully or dismissively.
* Terms require accepting significant risk (no deposit, no written agreement, unlimited liability) without any corresponding benefit.

Declining respectfully preserves the relationship for future opportunities better than accepting bad terms and delivering resentfully.

---

# Guiding Principle

A good negotiation ends with terms that are sustainable to deliver against — not just terms that win the client in the moment.
